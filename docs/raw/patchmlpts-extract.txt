Patch-MLP-TS: A Channel-Independent,
Attention-Free Architecture
for Long-Term Time Series Forecasting
Vedant Jadhav
Department of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning
Pimpri Chinchwad University
Pune, India
Email: vedantjadhav1414@gmail.com
Abstract—Transformer-based forecasters equipped with patch-
ing, such as PatchTST, have become strong baselines for long-
term time series forecasting, but their attention mechanism adds
computational cost that is not always matched by a proportional
accuracy gain over much simpler linear models. The patch-
based forecasting paradigm is revisited without attention, and
Patch-MLP-TS is presented: a channel-independent, all-MLP
architecture that combines patch embedding with cross-patch
(token-mixing) and per-patch (feature-mixing) MLP blocks, in
the spirit of MLP-Mixer. During development, a subtle but
consequential failure mode in a naive patch-plus-MLP design
is identified and corrected: without an explicit patch positional
embedding and a mechanism to mix information across patches,
a mean-pooled patch-MLP model is found to discard temporal
order entirely and is consistently outperformed by a simpler
whole-window baseline. After this correction, the resulting model
(v2) is found to be competitive in mean MSE with PatchTST and
DLinear on average across four ETT benchmarks (3-seed mean
MSE: 0.348 vs. 0.350 for PatchTST and 0.348 for DLinear); its
clearest advantage is shown at the longest forecasting horizon
on two of four datasets, and competitiveness with PatchTST
is demonstrated on the large-channel Electricity dataset (321
variates), with training achieved 1.5–1.8× faster and inference
latency reduced by up to 1.3× in the measured settings. Results
are reported with multi-seed variance, a set of ablations isolating
the contribution of channel-independence and patching, and an
efficiency analysis, and the settings where the method does not
win are explicitly reported, so that an accurate picture is given
of when a simple, attention-free patch-MLP design is and is not
preferable to attention-based or purely linear alternatives.
Index Terms—time series forecasting, multi-layer perceptron,
patching, channel independence, efficient architectures, Trans-
former alternatives
I. INTRODUCTION
Long-term time series forecasting (LTSF) underlies appli-
cations from energy load planning to traffic and financial
forecasting. Transformer-based architectures [1]–[3] were long
assumed to be the strongest approach to LTSF due to their
capacity to model long-range dependencies, but a family of
embarrassingly simple linear models (LTSF-Linear, including
DLinear and NLinear) was shown by Zeng et al. [4] to
outperform these Transformers on most standard benchmarks,
questioning whether attention is necessary for this task at all.
Subsequent work reconciled these findings by revisiting how
Transformers are applied to time series rather than abandoning
them. In PatchTST [5], each univariate channel is segmented
into patches and channel-independent attention is applied over
patch tokens, closing much of the gap to (and in several
cases surpassing) linear baselines. Around the same time, it
was demonstrated by MLP-Mixer-style architectures such as
TSMixer [6] that alternating time-mixing and feature-mixing
MLP layers, with no attention at all, can match Transformer-
level accuracy at substantially lower cost.
This raises a natural question: can the patching idea that
helps PatchTST be combined with a purely MLP backbone,
discarding attention entirely, without losing the accuracy
patching provides? A naive answer – in which each channel
is patch-embedded independently, a stack of per-patch MLP
blocks is applied, and the result is pooled – is found to fail
badly. It is shown that such a design, without a positional sig-
nal for patches and without any mechanism to mix information
across patches, is consistently and substantially outperformed
by an unpatched baseline with the same MLP capacity. This
failure mode is worth documenting explicitly, since it is easy
to overlook: it is not that patching is harmful in principle, but
that a patch representation with no notion of order and no
cross-patch interaction discards the very information patching
is supposed to preserve.
This is corrected with two additions – a learnable patch po-
sitional embedding and a token-mixing MLP layer that mixes
across patches, following the MLP-Mixer design [7] – and the
corrected model is referred to as Patch-MLP-TS (v2) through-
out this paper. It is evaluated against linear baselines (Linear,
NLinear, DLinear) and PatchTST across four ETT datasets and
the large-channel Electricity dataset, with three random seeds
used on ETT and two on Electricity, and ablations are reported
isolating the effect of channel-independence, patching, and
patch length, alongside a measured (not theoretical) efficiency
comparison against PatchTST.
The contributions of this work are as follows:
• A failure mode in naive patch-plus-MLP forecasting
architectures – loss of patch identity and order at the
pooling stage – is identified and documented, and it is

[Page 2]
shown that fixing it with positional embedding and cross-
patch mixing closes a previously large accuracy gap.
• A multi-seed benchmark against linear and attention-
based baselines is provided on four ETT datasets and
Electricity, with mean and standard deviation reported
rather than single-run numbers.
• Ablations are provided that isolate channel-independence
(large, consistent effect) and patching (smaller, horizon-
dependent effect), along with a patch-length sensitivity
study.
• Measured training and inference time are reported against
PatchTST, with a consistent training-time advantage
shown and a lower inference latency demonstrated on
the large-channel Electricity dataset, without reliance on
a parameter-count reduction.
Emphasis is placed on accuracy claims that are supported
by the data reported here rather than on a narrative of universal
superiority: the model is found to be competitive on average
and to win clearly in specific, identifiable settings, and the
settings where it does not win are also reported.
II. RELATED WORK
Linear and MLP-based forecasters. It was shown in
LTSF-Linear [4] that a single linear layer per channel, option-
ally combined with trend/seasonal decomposition (DLinear)
or last-value normalization (NLinear), matches or beats con-
siderably more complex Transformer forecasters on standard
LTSF benchmarks. This direction is extended by TSMixer
[6] with an all-MLP architecture that alternates time-mixing
and feature-mixing operations, in the style of MLP-Mixer [7],
and accuracy competitive with state-of-the-art Transformers is
reported.
Patch-based Transformers. In PatchTST [5], each channel
is segmented into overlapping patches, each patch is embedded
as a token, and a channel-independent Transformer encoder
is applied over the resulting patch sequence. The effective
receptive field per token is enlarged by patching, and the
quadratic cost of attention is reduced relative to point-wise
tokenization; channel-independence was shown to be impor-
tant for stable performance. A related but distinct approach is
taken by iTransformer [8], in which whole variates rather than
patches are tokenized and attention is applied across variates
instead of across time.
Position of this work. The patch tokenization of PatchTST
is combined with the attention-free, all-MLP mixing philos-
ophy of TSMixer, and what goes wrong when the two are
combined naively is specifically studied – a design point that,
to the authors’ knowledge, has not been explicitly documented
in prior work on patch-based forecasters.
III. METHOD
A. Problem Setup
Given a multivariate lookback window X ∈ RL×C with L
time steps and C channels, the forecasting task is to predict the
next H steps Y ∈ RH×C . Channel-independence is adopted
throughout: each of the C channels is processed independently
by a shared-weight model, and the per-channel outputs are
concatenated to form the final prediction.
B. Patch-MLP-TS Architecture
For a single channel x ∈ RL, the lookback window is split
into N = ⌊L/P ⌋ non-overlapping patches of length P , giving
xp ∈ RN ×P . Each patch is linearly embedded to dimension
d:
e = xpWpatch + bpatch, e ∈ RN ×d. (1)
A learnable positional embedding Epos ∈ RN ×d is added: e ←
e + Epos.
The embedded patches are passed through K mixer blocks.
In each block k, the following is applied, in sequence:
t = LayerNorm(e) (2)
t = MLPtoken(t⊤)⊤ (mix across the N patches) (3)
e ← e + t (4)
e ← e + MLPfeat(LayerNorm(e)) (mix across features)
(5)
where MLPtoken : RN → RN operates on the patch dimension
(with e transposed so that patches, not features, form the
last axis) and MLPfeat : Rd → Rd is a standard two-layer
feed-forward block with GELU activation, matching the time-
mixing / feature-mixing structure of MLP-Mixer [7].
After K blocks, a final LayerNorm is applied and all
N patch embeddings are flattened (not mean-pooled) and
projected to the forecast horizon:
ˆy = Flatten(e) Whead + bhead, ˆy ∈ RH . (6)
C. The v1 Failure Mode
In an earlier version of this architecture (v1), both the
positional embedding Epos and the token-mixing MLP were
omitted, with only feature-mixing blocks applied to each patch
independently and mean-pooling performed across patches
before the head. This design has no mechanism by which an
early patch can be distinguished from a late one, and whatever
order-dependent information survives the independent per-
patch processing is discarded by the mean-pool at the output.
Empirically, v1 was found to be outperformed by a no-
patching ablation (a single MLP over the whole lookback
window) in every one of 8 tested (dataset, horizon) settings on
ETTh1 and ETTh2, by margins as large as 2× in MSE. This
is reversed once the positional embedding and token-mixing
MLP are added (v2): the patched model is found to outperform
the no-patching ablation in 5 of the same 8 settings (Section
VI), and average accuracy across the full ETT benchmark
is improved from clearly the worst of five compared models
(mean rank 4.75/5) to competitive with the strongest baselines.
This transition is reported explicitly because the failure mode
– patch tokenization without any way to recover patch order or
mix across patches – is a natural design that might be arrived
at, and its consequences are judged severe enough to be worth
documenting.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A. Datasets
Evaluation is performed on the four standard ETT datasets
(ETTh1, ETTh2: hourly, 7 channels; ETTm1, ETTm2: 15-
minute, 7 channels) [1] and the Electricity dataset (hourly, 321
channels, UCI ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014). Data is
split chronologically 70%/10%/20% into train/validation/test,
with per-channel z-score normalization computed on the train-
ing split only.
B. Baselines
Comparisons are made against Linear and NLinear (single
linear layer, with and without last-value normalization) and
DLinear (trend/seasonal decomposition with two linear heads)
[4], and PatchTST [5], using a from-scratch reimplementation
with patch length 16, stride 8, model dimension 128, 8
attention heads, and 2 encoder layers. A lightweight TSMixer
[6] reproduction (hidden dimension 64, 2 mixer blocks) is ad-
ditionally reported as a single-seed, directional reference point;
this reproduction is not tuned to match published TSMixer
numbers and should not be read as a rigorous comparison.
C. Training Details
All models use lookback length L = 96 and are evaluated at
horizons H ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720} (ETT) and H ∈ {96, 720}
(Electricity, chosen to limit compute given the 321-channel
width). For Patch-MLP-TS, P = 12, d = 128, K = 3 mixer
blocks, and dropout 0.1 are used. All models are trained with
AdamW, learning rate 10−3, weight decay 10−4, batch size
64 (ETT) or 16–128 (Electricity, depending on model memory
footprint), for up to 20 epochs with early stopping (patience
5) on validation MSE, using mixed-precision training. Test-set
MSE and MAE are reported.
D. Seeds
ETT results are averaged over 3 seeds (42, 43, 44); Electric-
ity results are averaged over 2 seeds (42, 43) due to compute
constraints from its larger channel count. Mean ± standard
deviation is reported throughout, and the 2-seed Electricity
results are not presented as equivalent in rigor to the 3-seed
ETT results.
V. MAIN RESULTS
Test MSE (mean ± std) for all five models across four ETT
datasets and Electricity, at short (96) and long (720) horizons,
is reported in Table I. The full horizon sweep with error bars
is shown in Figure 1.
Across the 10 (dataset, horizon) settings in Table I, the
lowest mean MSE is attained by PatchTST in 6, by DLinear in
2, and by Patch-MLP-TS in 2. Averaged over the eight 3-seed
ETT rows, mean MSE is 0.3479 for DLinear, 0.3480 for Patch-
MLP-TS, and 0.3500 for PatchTST. Thus, an average MSE
nearly identical to DLinear is achieved by Patch-MLP-TS, and
competitiveness with PatchTST and plain Linear (0.3514) is
maintained on the ETT benchmarks.
The clearest, most seed-stable advantage for Patch-MLP-TS
is shown at long horizons on two of the four ETT datasets:
ETTh2 at H = 720 (0.2536 vs. 0.2633 for PatchTST and
0.2664 for DLinear) and ETTm1 at H = 720 (0.5346 vs.
0.5518 for PatchTST and 0.5495 for DLinear). Conversely,
ETTm1 at H = 96 is found to be the weakest setting for
both patch-based models, and is notably the least stable: a
std of 0.0146 is observed for Patch-MLP-TS and 0.0263 for
PatchTST at this setting, an order of magnitude larger than
DLinear’s 0.0006, while the simple linear baselines are won
outright by. ETTh2 at H = 96 and both Electricity horizons
are also won by PatchTST or DLinear. No diagnosis for the
ETTm1 H = 96 instability is offered here; it is flagged instead
as a direction for future work rather than explained away.
On Electricity specifically – the only dataset with a sub-
stantially larger channel count (321 vs. 7 for ETT) – near-
parity with PatchTST is achieved by Patch-MLP-TS at both
horizons (0.1766 vs. 0.1715 at H = 96; 0.2371 vs. 0.2315
at H = 720), while, as shown in Section VII, training and
inference are performed substantially faster. This is regarded
as the most practically relevant result in the paper: near-parity
accuracy with an attention-based model is achieved on a wide-
channel dataset, at lower wall-clock cost.
A comparison is additionally made against a lightweight,
untuned TSMixer reproduction at H ∈ {96, 720} across all
five datasets; it is outperformed by Patch-MLP-TS in all 10
settings tested. Given that this TSMixer implementation is
not tuned to match published numbers, this comparison is
presented as a directional data point rather than as a rigorous
comparison against the TSMixer architecture as originally
proposed.
VI. ABLATION STUDIES
A. Channel-Independence vs. Channel-Mixing
The default channel-independent Patch-MLP-TS is com-
pared against a variant in which all channels are mixed into
each patch token instead of being processed independently
(Table II, single seed).
A wide, consistent margin is won by channel-independence
in every setting tested – this is regarded as the single cleanest
and most confident finding in this ablation study, and it
matches the conclusion drawn for PatchTST [5]: independent
processing of the channels of a multivariate series with shared
weights is found to be more effective than mixing them at the
patch-embedding stage, at least for the datasets studied here.
B. Patching vs. No-Patching
The full patched model is compared against a variant using
the same mixer backbone but with no patch split (the entire
96-step lookback embedded as a single token), using the same
positional-embedding-free MLP stack (Table III, single seed).
5 of 8 settings are won by patching, and 3 are lost. The three
losses occur at H = 336 on ETTh1 and at H ∈ {336, 720} on
ETTh2, indicating that the benefit of patching is dataset- and
horizon-dependent rather than universal in this architecture.
Both ablations are shown together in Figure 2.

A consistent advantage in parameter count is not found for
Patch-MLP-TS: at H = 96 more parameters are used than by
PatchTST (499K vs. 416K), because the flatten-based output
head is scaled with N × d × H rather than with PatchTST’s
N ×dmodel ×H, which has a smaller effective N ×d product at
this horizon. At H = 720 this relationship is reversed (1.14M
vs. 1.38M, roughly 17% fewer parameters for Patch-MLP-TS),
because the two models’ patch counts and head dimensions are
scaled differently with horizon. A blanket parameter-efficiency
advantage is therefore not claimed.
What is observed consistently is a wall-clock speed ad-
vantage: training is performed 1.54× faster on ETTh1 and
1.76× faster on Electricity per epoch by Patch-MLP-TS, and
inference is performed 1.31× faster on Electricity (inference
on ETTh1 is performed marginally faster by PatchTST, 3.11ms
vs. 3.45ms). This speed advantage is maintained despite a
comparable or larger parameter count being used by Patch-
MLP-TS, and is attributed to the absence of the O(N 2)
attention computation rather than to a smaller model. This
is regarded as the more meaningful and more honestly sup-
portable efficiency claim than a parameter-count argument.
VIII. LIMITATIONS
These limitations are reported directly rather than omitted:
• 2 seeds, not 3, are used for the Electricity benchmark,
due to compute constraints from its 321-channel width;
its variance estimates are not presented as equivalent in
rigor to the 3-seed ETT results.
• The channel-mixing, no-patching, and patch-length abla-
tions (Tables II–IV) are each single-seed; variance esti-
mates are not available for these ablation numbers.
• Marked instability at H = 96 is observed for both patch-
based models on ETTm1 (standard deviation an order of
magnitude larger than the linear baselines at the same
setting); the cause has not been diagnosed and is flagged
as future work.
• The default patch length (P = 12) used throughout the
main benchmark is not the best-performing value at H =
96 on either ETTh1 or ETTh2 (Table IV); a full retuning
pass was judged out of scope for this study.
• The TSMixer baseline used here is a lightweight, untuned
reproduction and should not be read as a faithful repro-
duction of published TSMixer results.
• The main benchmark is won by Patch-MLP-TS in 2 of
10 (dataset, horizon) settings, and is lost in the remaining
8 to either PatchTST or DLinear; general superiority is
not claimed, only average competitiveness and specific,
identifiable wins at long horizons on two datasets and on
the large-channel Electricity dataset, in combination with
lower wall-clock cost.
IX. CONCLUSION
Patch-MLP-TS is presented: a channel-independent,
attention-free patch-based architecture for long-term time
series forecasting. A failure mode encountered during its
development is documented: a naive patch-embedding-plus-
MLP design with no positional signal and no cross-patch
mixing is consistently and substantially outperformed by a
simpler unpatched baseline, because information about patch
order is discarded by such a design. Once this is corrected
with a positional embedding and an MLP-Mixer-style token-
mixing layer, a model is obtained that is competitive in mean

[Page 6]
MSE with DLinear and PatchTST on average across four
ETT benchmarks, that wins clearly at the longest horizon on
two of those four datasets, and that remains competitive with
PatchTST on the substantially wider 321-channel Electricity
dataset. Faster training than PatchTST is also achieved by
Patch-MLP-TS in both measured settings, and lower inference
latency is achieved on Electricity, although faster inference
on ETTh1 is achieved marginally by PatchTST instead. These
efficiency gains are obtained without a consistent parameter-
count advantage, supporting the absence of self-attention
as the main computational distinction. Results are reported
with multi-seed variance where compute allowed, the settings
and ablations in which the method does not win are stated
explicitly, and the horizon-dependence of patching’s benefit –
rather than a single verdict of “patching helps” or “patching
hurts” – is regarded as itself a useful finding for future work
on patch-based, attention-free forecasting architectures.
